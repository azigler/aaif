# AAIF posting voice — canonical exemplars

> How Zig actually posts about AAIF: quirky, weird, fun, irreverent — meme as the
> vehicle, real teaching as the payload. Pairs with `brand-and-social.md` (the
> mechanics: badge, tags, hashtag) and the global `/zig-voice` skill (the full
> voice guide). When drafting any AAIF social post, match the register below, then
> run the `/zig-voice` self-audit.

## Why it works (the moves to reuse)

- **A meme is the delivery vehicle.** A recognizable pop-culture still + the AAIF
  logo does the hook; the copy plays off the image. (Same shape as the Kirby
  -inhaling-food GIF for the MCP-stateless post.) The meme makes it shareable;
  the copy makes it land.
- **The dialogue/setup-payoff format** ("THEM: …— / ME: …? yea, i am 💅💄") — a
  playful flex framing borrowed from the meme.
- **Glam-irreverent emoji register** — 💅💄🙌💖. Femme, fun, confident. Distinct
  from generic 🚀💡✨ corporate emojis.
- **Lowercase, slangy, warm.** "they got me gal," "dev darlings," "faves~," "ty …
  for the select." Trailing `~` for sing-song energy.
- **Warm network tag as a PS.** Tagging Rizel Scarlett with a real reason ("more
  excuses for livestreams 💖") — networking artifact + genuine warmth, not a
  transactional credit.
- **Confident ownership, zero apology.** No "humbled to announce," no "obligatory
  plug." Just the flex, played for fun.

## The rule for AAIF posts

**Quirky wrapper, real payload.** The silliness (meme + glam emojis + lowercase
flex) is the vehicle; underneath it there should be something genuinely useful —
a teaching hook, a real insight, a promise to do the homework for the audience.
The acceptance post's payload was "here's what I'll be working on + a warm tag."
A spec post's payload is "here's what actually changes and whether you need to
care." Never silliness with nothing underneath; never a dry teach with no fun.

Calibration knobs: meme/GIF as hook · glam-irreverent emoji set · lowercase +
slang ("u," "ur," "w/," "da comments," "y'all") · warm tags w/ a real reason ·
one ALL-CAPS word or two for emphasis · close on a peer question. Keep the AAIF
framing community-warm and vendor-neutral (the LinearB tie stays a closing link).

## Exemplar 3 — the launch post he rewrote from an agent draft (2026-10-01)

The post for the personal-site essay "From Muse to Genius to Factory"
(https://www.andrewzigler.com/feed/from-muse-to-genius-to-factory/), posted at
https://lnkd.in/p/g6fxARS6. aaif drafted 146 words; he hand-edited it to about 290 and
posted that. This is the most direct signal we have for a launch post: his text, diffed
against ours.

**What he posted (verbatim):**

> Most weeks it feels I'm searching for a lost city full of treasure, in the thickest jungle on the planet, covered in mosquito bites - "but I KNOW it must be out here, somewhere!"  🗺️
>
> Right now I have a spot marked on my map: "SOFTWARE FACTORY"
>
> chat, we're all hot on software factories rn. I even gave a LeadDev talk about this recently. But where are we _really_ trying to reach, when we set out into the jungle looking for "the factory"?
>
> "What do you mean, look for the factory? I'm going to build it."

>

>
> I've been chewing on how inspiration keeps changing address, and it plays an interesting role in that excavation. Back in my days studying Classics in college, I learned how Greeks called down a Muse, but the Romans HAD a genius, and somewhere around the Enlightenment we decided people ARE the genius, instead.

> Now, when someone runs a fleet of agents, we say they HAVE a factory, which is the same grammar the Romans used, when talking about their genius. Are we going back in time? Maybe... 😅

>
> https://lnkd.in/gwK4wcpp

**The diff against our draft:**
- KEPT, nearly word for word: "I've been chewing on how inspiration keeps changing address"; the Muse / HAD / ARE arc; "when someone runs a fleet of agents, we say they HAVE a factory, which is the same grammar the Romans used"; 😅.
- CUT: our hook, a clever detail (the Muses' "hallucination disclaimer", the Hesiod quote, the "inspire" etymology). He also folded our closing peer question ("What are y'all calling the thing you're building?") into the CTA ("or whatever you want to call it"), and dropped 📜 and 🐸.
- ADDED: a lost-city expedition scene as the opener, with his inner voice quoted; "chat," and "rn"; a cross-reference to his own LeadDev talk; a staged objection and reply ("I'm going to build it." / "Oh no... each factory has to be FOUND, actually."); the Yegge excavation quote; his personal backstory ("Back in my days studying Classics in college"); a stakes line ("AI shows up and shatters this norm"); a playful question after the grammar point ("Are we going back in time? Maybe..."); a CTA framed as the reader's own shift ("Flipping your creative instincts from building to discovery...").

**Lessons for the next launch-post draft:**
1. **The ONE insight is the article's thesis, not its cleverest detail.** We applied the go-deep-on-one-insight rule to a fun factoid. He went deep on the core turn (factories are found, not built) and dramatized it. Pick the idea the article exists to make.
2. **Open on a scene that IS the metaphor.** The jungle expedition carries the excavation thesis before it is ever stated. A "fun fact" opener is a hook; a scene is the argument in costume.
3. **Stage the objection as dialogue, then correct it playfully.** The quoted "I'm going to build it." plus "Oh no... I'm sorry to inform you" is his teaching move: voice the reader's assumption, then flip it warmly.
4. **"Short and sweet" means ~250-300 words to him, not ~150.** He expanded our draft by about 2x. This matches the 2026-09-04 teaser finding: he writes longer and warmer than agents assume.
5. **Cross-reference his own recent work** (the LeadDev talk) and credit the thinker whose words he builds on (Yegge), in the post itself.
6. **The CTA names the reader's transformation** ("from building to discovery") and absorbs the peer question.
7. **His register is louder than the skill's defaults.** He uses ALL CAPS on 7 words (KNOW, SOFTWARE FACTORY, FOUND, HAD, ARE, BEING, HAVE), streamer slang ("chat,"), an underscore-italic (_really_), and a spaced hyphen as a dash. Don't "correct" these in his own text. Its typos ("excavation your own") are his to keep or fix.

## Long-form rules — Zig's review of an agent-drafted essay (2026-10-17)

An agent drafted the October essay as a guided tour of the agent system it runs in. Zig rejected it
and asked for a rewrite. His review is the standing brief for every long-form piece. Each rule below
is his point, made checkable.

1. **Arrive with one purpose.** The piece must know what it is for before line one. A draft that
   discovers its subject while you read it is journaling, not writing. Write it as a report sent
   back from the future, not as musing.
2. **Do not talk down or force awe.** No nudge-nudge, no "this is a bigger deal than you think".
   Say the thing simply and exactly, and let the reader decide it is remarkable. Prose that pushes
   the reader toward wonder pushes them away.
3. **The reader was not on the journey.** Our own scale, counts and achievements mean nothing to a
   stranger. Every claim has to give the reader a reason to care, on their own terms.
4. **Have references, but do not wear them.** No explicit timestamps, frame numbers or tallies as
   anchors ("8 of 14 kept"). Having the data is not a reason to print it.
5. **No guided tour.** No "stop one, stop two" and no extended physical-space metaphor for the
   system. Metaphor that maps every part to a room makes the piece hard to follow, and it teaches
   nothing.
6. **Cut AI filler on sight.** Example of what he flagged: "A lot of what I'll show you, I found by
   walking into a room and asking what something was for." A line that only gestures at a story is
   slop. A quote from a named builder earns its place only if the next sentence makes it clearer,
   not more confusing.
7. **The AAIF tie is the spine, never a bolt-on.** If the project link (A2A, MCP, AGENTS.md) could
   be cut without breaking the argument, the piece is built wrong.
8. **One piece, one job.** If it tries to cover the protocol, the promises, attention and the place
   all at once, it hits none of them.
9. **Give the reader something to work with.** Express where agent work is going, and show how
   agents collaborate, in a form a developer can try.
10. **Grade it cold before it reaches Zig.** Two smart models at high or max effort read the draft
    cold and grade writing and voice: is it interesting, cutting edge, on the frontier, informative
    from the front lines (not smoke in the ears), and does the reader have a reason to care? The
    grades travel with the draft.

### Second review (2026-10-18): accurate is not enough

Zig read two rewrites that followed the ten rules above and rejected both. They were correct and
dry. These rules add to the ten; none of them cancels one.

11. **Wonder, earned.** Slow down at the moments that are genuinely strange. Say how unnatural the
    new way of working feels and which old assumption broke, so the reader is on an adventure with
    you. A factual log of what the system did is not an essay. Rule 2 still holds: earn the wonder
    with a concrete moment, never with adjectives or announcements.
12. **Concepts over mechanics.** Teach the idea, not the query. A runnable join or a JSON record is
    boring unless the concept needs it. Carry the concept so a reader can rebuild it in their own
    system.
13. **Sharpen the braindumps; do not list them.** Zig's braindumps are raw material. The drafting
    model's job is to work out what he is trying to say and say it better. Pasting each idea in as a
    marked paragraph is not sharpening it.
14. **Set up the big frame.** When the subject is a new way to build (the factory as a place, ideas
    borrowed from MUDs), spend real space on why it matters. Build on his own earlier writing where
    it fits. Rule 5 bans the tour, not the idea of place: argue why a place is the right shape, and
    do not walk the reader through rooms.
15. **Read more references than you cite.** A draft that read only its own facts file sounds like
    one. Read his earlier essays and the writers he names, and match the register he points at.

## Reference voice: Felix Rieseberg, "How to be more ambitious" (2026-10-13)

Zig named this piece as voice inspiration for aaif ("great voice inspo for aaif's project too"),
and routed it here on 2026-10-25 from the lab's ad hoc reading walk.
Source: https://felixrieseberg.com/how-to-be-more-ambitious-a-tutorial-for-humans-with-models/

The moves to borrow:

1. **One familiar example, extended step by step.** He takes an ordinary quarterly business
   review and moves it from filling in a template, to finding the decisions, to redesigning the
   review itself. Each step has a clear reason to exist. This is rule 12 (concepts over
   mechanics) done well: the reader rebuilds the idea on their own work.
2. **Headings that state the turn, in everyday words.** Specimens: "We have been here before",
   "Not reading the code", "The view outside", "Some closing thoughts". No labels, no puns.
3. **Terms, then a sample prompt, then his own project.** He defines a word, shows the prompt a
   reader could paste, then grounds it in work he did. Advice and evidence alternate.
4. **A strong claim travels with its boundary.** He says where his advice fits less well and
   that outside validation is still needed. Give the reader room to disagree.

A pre-draft check, from his four directions (speed, altitude, depth, range). Before a piece
starts, write down: the artifact asked for; the outcome the reader should reach (altitude); the
raw sources, the expert method and the check that could disprove the main claim (depth); and
which other field has a method that sharpens the explanation (range). A bigger piece is not a
deeper one. Depth needs a stronger method and an independent check, not more scope.
